Athena's Children

A Yale Computing History

In November 2025, a man named Shawn stood on Aventine Hill in Rome, looking out over the ancient city. He was there because, decades earlier, he had named a server after this place.

This is a story about the people who named computers after gods and hills and sins, and the strange kind of immortality that comes from infrastructure.

Athena's Children

In the 1980s, MIT created Project Athena—a distributed computing environment that would change how universities thought about academic computing. The name was fitting: Athena, goddess of wisdom, patron of learning.

At Yale, someone decided to follow in those footsteps. But Yale had its own character, its own sense of classical tradition. If MIT had claimed the Greek Athena, Yale would honor her Roman counterpart.

And so the first server was named Minerva.

The Pantheon

One server became many. The pattern emerged: Roman gods, starting with M.

  • Minerva — goddess of wisdom, the first
  • Mercury — messenger, swift
  • Morpheus — god of dreams (Greek, but the M was irresistible)
  • Mars — god of war, guardian

They called this constellation "The Pantheon." Servers that faced users, that bore the weight of daily work, that carried the names of gods.

The Seven Hills

Rome was built on seven hills. Behind Yale's public-facing servers, the backend infrastructure needed names too.

Someone—possibly Aleks Margan, a Slovenian systems administrator who knew the weight of classical names—chose the hills of Rome:

  • Capitoline — the most sacred hill, home to temples
  • Aventine — the hill of the plebeians, named by Shawn

Fileservers. Silent, essential, the foundation upon which everything else was built. Like hills.

Janus

The mail server presented a unique problem. It had to look in two directions: toward the backend systems and toward the users. It was a gateway, a threshold.

There was only one appropriate name: Janus, the two-faced god of doorways and transitions, who looked simultaneously backward and forward, inward and outward.

It was perfect.

The Seven Sins

The routers—seven of them—needed names. Joe Paolillo, the story goes, looked at the number and had an idea that would be, as one observer noted, "more controversial today."

The seven deadly sins:

  • sloth
  • pride
  • wrath
  • envy
  • lust
  • gluttony
  • greed

It was cheeky. It was memorable. It was the kind of decision that could only be made in a time when infrastructure was still intimate, when the people who named the machines were the same people who maintained them.

The People

Names are stories, but names are also people.

Aleks Margan, from Slovenia, who may have been the one to choose Capitoline. A systems administrator who understood that infrastructure deserved gravitas.

Joe Paolillo, who possibly named the routers after sins, understanding that a little irreverence makes the work bearable.

Shawn, who named Aventine and then, decades later, stood on the actual hill in Rome, closing a loop that began with a server rack in New Haven.

Their names are not on monuments. But their choices echo through decades of uptime.

Legacy

These servers are gone now. Replaced, upgraded, retired. The names have been recycled or forgotten. This is the nature of infrastructure: nothing lasts, but everything matters.

But somewhere, in backup logs and old documentation and the memories of systems administrators who have since moved on, these names persist. Minerva. Capitoline. Janus. Sloth.

We name things to remember them. We name things to honor them. We name things because even in the most technical work, we cannot help but reach for poetry.

This is the small monument. The digital plaque. The story that deserves to be told.

Here stood servers. Here stood gods.
